Sensors that report change

Point a conventional camera and an event camera at the same scene and the difference is immediate. One waits for the shutter and hands back a picture. The other reports every change as it happens, pixel by pixel, in microseconds.

See the recorded work

An event camera produces nothing at all when nothing moves. Which makes a still photograph the wrong way to show this work.

The centre studies biological brains and engineers their advantages into hardware: sensors that report change instead of capturing frames, processors that compute in spikes, and the algorithms between them. The output is temporal, so the recordings below are the work itself rather than illustrations of it.

Separating a scene by motion alone

Objects close to you cross the frame faster than distant ones when you are moving, as in a car or a train. That difference alone pulls them apart, with no recognition step.

Many sensors, one sensor

The high temporal resolution of event-based sensors lets several of them fuse seamlessly into a single larger sensor, covering a wider line without a stitching step.

Sensiball

A robot that tracks and returns a ball faster than a frame camera could see it. The same closed loop is what a fast line needs to catch a defect and act on it.

What you gain, in two minutes

Latency, dynamic range, power and data volume, stated plainly. The clip to send a colleague who asks why this is not just a faster camera.

Learning onboard, in flight

Recorded from a drone over Telluride, Colorado. Raw events are rendered on an exponentially decaying time surface, with the map learned on the platform rather than off it.

Agriculture & environment

Counting and classifying living things in cluttered outdoor scenes, on battery, with no network.

Separating what moves from what drifts

The telescope holds the moon still on a cloudy night and the cloud separates from it by velocity alone. The same separation finds an insect against moving vegetation.

In the field, New South Wales

Counting what moves

Picking a bee out of moving foliage by how it moves rather than how bright it is, at wingbeat rates a frame camera cannot resolve. The same method finds a pest in a greenhouse.

Tools & data

Take it and use it

All repositories →

The centre writes and maintains the software it uses, and publishes it openly. Several of these are depended on by groups elsewhere. If you want to try event-based vision without buying a camera, start with the simulator.

Python

IEBCS

The centre’s event-based camera simulator. Generate realistic event streams from conventional footage, so you can develop against the sensor before you own one.

Rust & Python

neuromorphic-drivers

Talk to event cameras in real time from Python or Rust, without the vendor stack getting in the way.

Rust

aedat

A fast decoder for AEDAT 4, the recording format most event-camera data arrives in.

Python

astrometry

Turns a list of star positions into a pixel-to-sky transformation. The plate-solving step behind the sky-mapping footage above.

Rust

sgp4

An implementation of the satellite propagation model used to predict where an orbiting object will be. The most widely used thing the centre publishes.

Dataset index

land

A maintained list of the neuromorphic datasets available anywhere, not only the centre’s own. Where to start if you need data rather than hardware.

Study with us

Work with us on this

Write to the centre →

Recruitment here is continuous. There is no closing date and no application window.

The list below is directions we would supervise, not vacancies that open and close. Most of them scope to any of the three levels: a capstone takes a slice, a masters project takes a stage, a doctorate takes the whole question. Send a page describing what you would want to do and who you would want to work with, and we will tell you honestly whether it fits.

Start a conversation

Capstone

Final-year undergraduate project

A scoped slice of live work: one sensor, one dataset, one question, finished within the session. The cheapest way to find out whether research suits you, and several of ours have turned into masters places.

Open to any Western Sydney final-year engineering or computing student.

Masters

Master of Applied Neuromorphic Engineering

A world-first degree spanning electrical engineering, computer science, neuroscience and mathematics, taught by the people building the field, with either a coursework or a research pathway.
