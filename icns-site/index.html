International Centre for Neuromorphic Systems · Western Sydney University

Sensors that report change

Every pixel decides for itself when something happened. Hold a telescope on the moon through cloud and the two separate on their own, because they cross the sensor at different speeds. No frames, no exposure to choose.

See the recorded work

An event camera produces nothing at all when nothing moves. Which makes a still photograph the wrong way to show this work.

The centre studies biological brains and engineers their advantages into hardware: sensors that report change instead of capturing frames, processors that compute in spikes, and the algorithms between them. The output is temporal, so the recordings below are the work itself rather than illustrations of it.

Dynamic range·26 May 2021

A full lunar eclipse, both halves visible at once

The illuminated and the eclipsed portions of the moon stay clear together. A conventional sensor exposes for the brighter lit area and loses the rest, because it has one exposure to spend and every pixel must share it.

Space situational awareness

Simultaneous sky mapping and satellite tracking

A star map builds continuously from the stars passing through the field of view. Their positions then reveal the motion of the telescope itself.

Perception

Seeing the moon through cloud

The telescope holds the moon still on a cloudy night. Because the cloud and the moon move at different velocities, the sensor separates them.

Algorithms

Separating a scene by motion alone

Objects close to you cross the frame faster than distant ones when you are moving, as in a car or a train. That difference alone is enough to pull them apart.

Self-organising maps on a flying drone

Recorded from a drone over Telluride, Colorado. The raw events are rendered on an exponentially decaying time surface.
