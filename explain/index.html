How to fit a whole world into one list of numbers

I teach robots to remember places a bit like brains might: no databases, no grid maps — just very long vectors and three algebra moves. Here's the whole idea, with toys to poke.

scroll ↓
01 · FINGERPRINTS

Every thing gets a fingerprint

Forget neat little boxes. In my world, a concept — mug, dog, kitchen — is a long list of random numbers. Hundreds or thousands of them. That list is the concept's fingerprint, and here it's drawn as a colourful barcode.

Random sounds careless. It's the whole trick: randomness guarantees every fingerprint is unmistakably itself.

mug = [0.2, −0.9, 0.4, … ] × 256

02 · ELBOW ROOM

In high dimensions, everyone's a stranger

In 2-D, two random arrows often point roughly the same way. Add dimensions and that stops: by a few thousand, random fingerprints are almost exactly perpendicular — overlap hovering near zero.

Slide the dimension count and watch the accidental-overlap pile squeeze onto zero. That squeeze is what lets one system hold millions of concepts without a filing system.

03 · THE BAG

Adding = tossing things in a bag

Add fingerprints together and you get a new vector that behaves like a bag: it resembles everything inside it, and nothing outside it.

One vector now stands for a whole collection — and you can still check membership just by measuring overlap. No list. No lookup. Just geometry.

bag = mug + keys + dogoverlap(bag, mug) ≈ 58%

04 · THE GLUE

Multiplying = glue that dissolves on demand

Binding glues two fingerprints into a brand-new one — kitchen ⊛ mug, "the mug that lives in the kitchen". The child resembles neither parent. It's a fresh stranger.

Here's the light math: each number in the vector is an angle on a little dial, and binding just adds angles, dial by dial. Which means it un-does perfectly — subtract the kitchen's angles and the mug walks out unharmed.

θ_pair = θ_kitchen + θ_mugpair ⊘ kitchen = mug

05 · THE PAYOFF

A whole scene, one vector

Now combine the moves. Glue each thing to its place, toss the pairs in a bag:

memory = kitchen⊛mug + sofa⊛dog + garden⊛ball

That single vector is the robot's memory of the whole scene. To ask it a question, unbind a place: out comes a noisy fingerprint, and you recognise it the way you'd recognise your dog in a blurry photo — nearest match wins.

No search through records. Asking is just arithmetic.

06 · SO WHAT?

Where my research comes in

These are vector symbolic algebras, and my work puts them on robots. Two upgrades make it real: places become smooth coordinates instead of separate fingerprints (spin the dials by how far you've moved), and a camera's neural network learns to produce the fingerprints directly from pixels.

The result — presented at ICRA 2026 — is a robot that answers "where's the mug?" with algebra instead of a database: one vector for the world, three moves to use it.
